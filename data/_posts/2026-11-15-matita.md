The word for "pencil" in Indo-European languages shows interesting differences that intersect with the history of how this humble instrument has become what it is today. We will only look at some of these languages, and very briefly though!

# Spanish & Portuguese

These languages use respectively "lápiz" and "lápis", directly derived from the original Latin "lapis", which means ["stone"](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/lapis), and stones have historically been used for writing and drawing before any specific implement was invented. We'd go down another rabbit hole, but ["lapis lazuli"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapis_lazuli) (which in Italian became "lapislazzuli") is the vivid blue stone extensively used in jewellery and also to extract the pigment.

# Italian

Italian uses ["matita"](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matita), again of Latin origin and picking from a specific type of stone used in mark-making, the "lapis haematitis", an iron-rich mineral that produced a quintessentially reddish tint so typical of Reinassance drawings - note that "haematitis" refers to the Greek-derived semantical sphere of "blood".

# German 

Pencil in German is "Bleistift", literally "lead pen" - for a long time before graphite was discovered as a mineral and subsequently packaged in thin sticks wrapped in a case, artists used pens with points made of metal (a "stylus"). Said metal was usually lead. In the 1500s, graphite was discovered as a naturally-occurring mineral and started getting used for writing, but people initially thought it was a form of lead, so the [name](https://samueljohnsonbirthplace.org.uk/pencil-day/) stuck, and German apparently kept it.

# French

French uses ["crayon"](https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A9C4840), meaning a form of soil or earth, which points to a generic, not well-specified mineral.

# English

English's ["pencil"](https://www.etymonline.com/search?q=pencil) comes from Old French "pencel". French lost this, English kept it, fascinating.
